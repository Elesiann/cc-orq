## orq tools

These tools start, read and finish orq jobs, the background runs of Codex, OpenCode, Antigravity and Claude CLIs. Every job has a `taskId`, the job id.

- Call `capabilities` before you name a provider instance or a model. It lists each configured instance, its live models, each model's options and allowed values, and its constraints. Do not guess model ids. Its `runtimeMode` and `interactionMode` are the most a task in its own worktree may run as: the lower of the config's `ceiling` and the session's permission mode, which counts as far as the cc-orq hook supplies it; without the hook only the config's `ceiling` holds. `ceiling` in the answer shows each limit and the lower ceiling of a `shared` task.
- `delegate` starts a job. It takes `target` (an instance id), `model`, `cwd` (absolute, inside the session directory or an allowed root) and `prompt`, and optionally `effort`, `isolation`, `role`, `runtime` and `interaction`. The worker has no history, so the prompt carries the whole brief, at most 200,000 characters: the goal, the files it may touch and the check that shows it worked. `isolation` is `worktree` by default, a new branch in its own git worktree: tell the worker to commit, because `integrate` merges commits only. Use `shared` only for a task that edits nothing. With MCP tasks the answer is a background task that `/tasks` shows, and the session wakes by itself with the result: end your turn after delegating, and do not poll. A client without tasks gets `{"taskId", "status": "running"}`, and you read the job with `status` when you are ready for it, not in a tight loop. Leave `runtime` and `interaction` out to get the ceiling. A request above it opens a form where the user can allow it for that task only, when the client has tasks and forms. A declined form, or a client without forms, ends in `runtime_mode_escalation_denied` or `interaction_mode_escalation_denied`, and the refusal says what would lift the limit: ask for less, or tell the user, and do not retry other modes to find one that passes. `questions` is `parent`; `human` shows the user a form for the worker's questions, and the same task goes on with their answer. A declined form, or one open for 24 hours, ends the task with `BLOCKED`, and then `answer` works.
- `answer` takes `{"taskId": "<id>", "answer": "<text>"}` for a job whose result is `BLOCKED on a question`. Settle the question yourself when the brief and the conversation do, and ask the user when they do not. The same worker resumes its own session, in its worktree and on its branch, as a new task: end your turn, as after `delegate`. A second answer gets `already_answered`, naming the continuation. `resume_unavailable` says why the worker cannot resume, and then `delegate` again with the brief and the answer.
- `status` takes `{"taskId": "<id>"}`, the id that `/tasks` shows, and answers one job's state: `running`, `waiting`, `completed`, `failed`, `cancelled` or `interrupted`. Once the job has ended, it carries the `result` or the `error`. It works for jobs of earlier sessions too. A job that stopped on a question has `BLOCKED on a question` as its result.
- `list` shows the newest jobs first, whoever started them. Pass `{"limit": N}` for more or fewer than 20, up to 50. Use it to find the `taskId` of an older job, then read that job with `status`.
- `cancel` takes `{"taskId": "<id>"}` and an optional `reason`. It stops a running or waiting job, which then reads `cancelled`. A job that has ended answers `task_not_cancellable`.
- `integrate` with `{"taskId": "<id>"}` merges a finished worktree job's branch into the checkout the job was launched from. It merges commits only, so the worker must have committed. When it answers `not_fast_forward`, call it again with `"strategy": "merge"`. When it answers `merge_conflict`, give a rebase of the job's branch to the worker that wrote it, then integrate again. The other refusals say what to fix.
